The Newsroom Design Lab

A finding says what is wrong. The lab shows what right could look like. Built 11 to 13 October 2026, it is four SGit vaults, each a self-contained app, that try the fixes from the newsroom review on the real newsroom data (a byte-faithful snapshot of 73 articles), in three design directions each, so that a direction can be chosen by looking rather than by arguing. Nothing in the lab changes the live site.

Every view in the lab says which step of the reader's path it moves them to: arrive through a link someone sent, read and rate, come back, ask, act.

Three directions

A direction is a different design, not a recolour: different hierarchy, type, density and interaction, over the same data. Every vault has a switch in its top bar, and a Compare view that shows all three at phone width.

A · Broadsheet

A serious publication. Editorial hierarchy, one lead, a named author with a stated purpose.

Serif headlines, warm paper, rules between columns, kickers, restrained colour. FT / Guardian long-read feel.

B · Briefing

Answers first. Every unit opens with the news in two sentences, then labelled blocks: the news, our view, the evidence, room for disagreement.

Sans, compact cards with reading time and format, bold labels, one accent colour. Semafor / Axios feel.

C · Your desk

The reader's own newsroom. The persona is the front page; the board, highlights, notes and questions are first-class.

App-like: left rail, side column, chips and progress. Readwise / Arc feel.

The decisions it exists to settle

The lab's hub vault lets the editor pick one option per decision, kept in the browser and downloadable as JSON. These are the questions, as the lab asks them.

  1. Which direction should the live front page take? Everything else follows from it: type, density, how much the reader's own data shows.
    • A · Broadsheet
    • B · Briefing
    • C · Your desk
    • Mix: A for the publication, C for a signed-in reader
  2. Which masthead line names the heart? The tagline today describes a method, not a subject.
    • Field notes on AI agents, security and the next generation of publishing, by Dinis Cruz
    • Open thinking on agentic AI: what to build, how to secure it, and how to ship it
    • The agentic era, written in the open
  3. How many doors on the first screen? Three is easier to choose from; five covers everyone you send links to.
    • Three (security, builders, news)
    • Five personas plus 'just looking'
  4. What opens every article? Readers have three minutes on a phone; the median article is about 4,500 words.
    • A boxed 'In brief' (the Explainer's short version)
    • Labelled blocks: the news, our view, the evidence, room for disagreement
    • The article with the reader's side column
  5. How should a rating reach you? Today it stays in the reader's browser unless they send their whole reading.
    • One tap, anonymous, with plain consent
    • One tap, with an optional email for a reply
    • Kept locally, sent weekly with the reader's consent
  6. Desks by audience or by subject? Topics today overlap and one holds 38 of 73 articles.
    • Five desks by audience (proposed)
    • Keep the six topics, add tag pages
  7. Ship the reading board in the first release? It gives a returning reader a reason to come back, and it is the most app-like piece.
    • Yes, on every persona page
    • Later, once ratings flow

How it is built

A shared kit (v0.1.3, vendored into each vault): native web components with no framework, a bundler that packs each app into one index.html (the vault-app contract), an escaping template every view renders through, a router that never assigns location.hash inside the host, a reader store that uses the host's sg.state because the frame refuses localStorage, and a release gate that renders every route in every direction at 390 and 1280 px and fails on any page error, any colour outside the theme file, or any sideways scroll on a phone. Built by a lead with an architect agent and three developer agents, one vault each.

Reader data in the prototypes stays in the browser. Every "send" button is simulated, and says so, showing the receipt the real encrypted append lane would give.
